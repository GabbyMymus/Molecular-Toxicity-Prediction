\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Molecular toxicity prediction uses chemical representations and machine
learning models to identify compounds that may trigger biological
responses. Our study builds on prior research in toxicity benchmarking,
molecular fingerprints, chemical descriptors, generalization evaluation,
and class-imbalanced classification. These foundations motivate the
comparison of classical machine learning models in Phase 1 and
graph-based learning approaches in Phase 2.

\subsection{Toxicity Benchmarks and Challenges}

The Tox21 Data Challenge established a benchmark for predicting compound
activity across 12 toxicity-related bioassays covering nuclear receptor
and stress response pathways. DeepTox demonstrated the effectiveness
of machine learning pipelines combining chemical features and predictive
models for toxicity classification \cite{mayr2016deeptox}. MoleculeNet
subsequently provided a standardized framework for evaluating molecular
machine learning methods, including Tox21, and highlighted the importance
of dataset partitioning and task-appropriate evaluation metrics
\cite{wu2018moleculenet}.

Tox21 presents two important challenges: missing labels and class
imbalance. A compound may not have been tested in every assay; therefore,
an unavailable label must not be interpreted as a negative result.
Furthermore, active compounds constitute a minority of labeled samples
for many endpoints. Accordingly, models must be trained and evaluated
using only the available labels for each endpoint.

\subsection{Molecular Representations}

Extended-Connectivity Fingerprints (ECFP), described by Rogers and Hahn
\cite{rogers2010extended}, represent molecules through their local atomic
environments. Morgan fingerprints encode these environments as
fixed-length binary vectors using hashing. In this study, radius-2
Morgan fingerprints with 2048 bits are used, corresponding to the
ECFP4 representation.

Molecular descriptors provide complementary information by summarizing
whole-molecule properties, such as molecular weight, lipophilicity,
topological polar surface area, and hydrogen-bonding characteristics.
Unlike fingerprints, which represent local structural environments,
descriptors capture global physicochemical and structural properties.
We therefore evaluate Morgan fingerprints alone and their combination
with molecular descriptors to investigate the contribution of these
complementary feature representations.

\subsection{Generalization and Scaffold Splits}

Bemis and Murcko introduced molecular frameworks that identify core
structural scaffolds within compounds \cite{bemis1996properties}.
In molecular machine learning, scaffold-based splitting groups molecules
according to their Bemis--Murcko scaffolds and assigns scaffold groups
to separate dataset partitions. This reduces the possibility of closely
related molecular frameworks appearing in both training and evaluation
sets.

Random splitting can place structurally similar compounds in different
partitions, potentially producing optimistic estimates of generalization
to new chemical families. MoleculeNet discusses scaffold splitting as a
more challenging alternative for evaluating molecular machine learning
models \cite{wu2018moleculenet}. Accordingly, our experiments compare
random and scaffold splits to examine how the choice of partitioning
affects predictive performance on Tox21.

\subsection{Evaluation Under Class Imbalance}

Accuracy can be misleading in imbalanced classification because a model
may achieve a high score by favoring the majority class while failing
to identify active compounds. We therefore use the Receiver Operating
Characteristic Area Under the Curve (ROC AUC) and Precision--Recall
Area Under the Curve (PR AUC) to evaluate model performance.

ROC AUC measures a model's ability to rank positive samples above
negative samples. Precision--recall analysis focuses on the relationship
between precision and recall and is particularly informative when the
positive class is rare. Saito and Rehmsmeier discuss the usefulness of
precision--recall curves for interpreting classifier performance on
imbalanced datasets \cite{saito2015precision}. Unlike the ROC AUC
reference level of 0.5 for random ranking, the baseline Average
Precision of a random-ranking classifier is approximately equal to the
positive-class prevalence.

In our experiments, both metrics are calculated separately for each
toxicity endpoint, excluding samples with missing labels. Class weights
are also used where appropriate to reduce the influence of class
imbalance during model training.

\subsection{Learned Graph Representations and Phase 2 Motivation}

Traditional molecular machine learning relies on predefined chemical
representations, such as fingerprints and molecular descriptors.
Graph neural networks provide an alternative by representing atoms as
nodes and chemical bonds as edges, allowing molecular representations
to be learned from graph structure.

Gilmer et al. introduced the Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN)
framework, in which atoms iteratively aggregate information from their
neighbors and update their representations before a molecular-level
prediction is produced \cite{gilmer2017neural}. Their study evaluated
molecular property prediction on QM9, a quantum-chemistry dataset,
rather than Tox21 toxicity classification. Its findings therefore
motivate the investigation of learned molecular representations but do
not establish that graph neural networks will outperform classical
models on our task.

Phase 1 establishes classical machine learning baselines using Logistic
Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost with Morgan fingerprints and
molecular descriptors. The resulting evaluation framework provides a
basis for a subsequent comparison with graph-based models in Phase 2,
using consistent endpoint definitions and evaluation procedures.
